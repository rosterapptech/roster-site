**In short:** There is no single best dating app for polyamory. There are apps built around non-monogamy from the ground up (Feeld, #open), apps with broad reach and usable filters (OkCupid), and apps where you will permanently explain why your profile does not fit the template (Tinder, Hinge, Bumble). Which one suits you depends less on the feature list than on the size of your city, how out you are, and what you are actually looking for.

Anyone living polyamorously who wants to meet new people runs into a problem monogamous dating advice never covers: most apps are built on the assumption that you will eventually find one person and delete the app. Several simultaneous, equally valid relationships are simply not part of their logic, and you notice it in every other profile field.

This article sorts out which apps are genuinely built for non-monogamous dating, where the trade-offs sit, and how to tell within five minutes whether an app accounts for you or merely tolerates you.

*Last checked: September 2026. Dating app features and pricing change frequently – the current details are always in the App Store and on each provider's own site.*

## The three categories every dating app falls into

Before the individual names, a distinction helps. Almost every app worth considering for polyamorous dating belongs to one of these three groups.

**Built for non-monogamy.** The app assumes people can have several connections. Relationship structure is a regular profile field, not a footnote. Partnerships can be made visible. The advantage lies less in the features than in the baseline mood: you do not have to explain yourself.

**Tolerance with features.** Large, general apps that explicitly include non-monogamous people and offer fields and filters for them. The reach is considerably bigger, the community more mixed. You will more often meet people for whom polyamory is new.

**Not accounted for.** The mainstream apps. No meaningful field for your relationship structure, an algorithm optimised for exclusive matching, and an audience that is mostly looking for monogamy. Usable, but tiring.

## Feeld: the default for non-monogamous dating

Feeld describes itself as a dating app for open-minded people and cites a community of over 14 million users. For polyamorous people, one feature matters most: profiles can be linked with up to five other people, so existing connections are visible to anyone you match with, without you having to explain them individually in every chat. There is also a group chat where several people involved can talk in one place.

Ethical non-monogamy is a regular profile attribute, and gender and orientation go far beyond the usual two or three options. Basic use is free, and the paid tier is called Majestic Membership.

**Where it pays off:** If you live in a larger city and want to meet people you no longer have to explain polyamory to. The linking feature is the most honest way to make an existing partnership visible.

**The catch:** In smaller towns and rural areas you will exhaust the pool quickly. Feeld also leans noticeably towards short-term and sexual encounters, so anyone looking for a third or fourth long-term relationship needs to filter more patiently.

## #open: smaller, closer to the community, with real-world events

#open positions itself as a social network for non-monogamy rather than just a dating app. The platform supports nine different relationship types and separates solo and partnered profiles, which is particularly practical for people who date differently depending on context. A larger share of its users are already in a relationship, a smaller share are searching solo. On top of that come partnerships with real-world venues across the US and Canada.

**Where it pays off:** If community matters more to you than raw match counts, and if you want fine-grained control over your visibility.

**The catch:** The centre of gravity is clearly North America. Outside it, user density is noticeably thinner than on Feeld, and the focus leans towards the swinging and lifestyle side of non-monogamy rather than long-term polyamory.

## OkCupid: the pragmatic choice where user density is low

OkCupid is not a poly app, but it has held a place in the scene for years. Relationship structure is part of the profile data, and the extensive matching questions surface early what would otherwise only come up in the third conversation: attitudes to jealousy, to exclusivity, to metamours. Answering them precisely saves you a lot of conversations that were never going to work.

**Where it pays off:** If you live outside major cities. Reach is the real advantage here, not the feature list. In a town of 60,000 people you will realistically find more matches here than in a niche app.

**The catch:** You are meeting a largely monogamous audience. Expect more explaining, and expect people for whom your profile is the first time they have thought about polyamory at all.

## Tinder, Hinge and Bumble: possible, but work

The big mainstream apps have the widest reach and the worst fit. Their profile structure has no meaningful field for several simultaneous relationships, their algorithms are optimised for exclusive matching, and the response to an openly polyamorous profile is often blunt.

If you use them anyway: put your relationship structure in the first two lines of the free text, not at the end. Anything else means you end up mentioning it in the chat, which reads to the other person like a withheld detail, and lands you in exactly the debate you wanted to avoid. How to phrase it so it does not sound like fine print is covered in [Telling a date you are polyamorous](/en/blog/disclosing-polyamory-dating/).

## The four questions that let you judge an app in five minutes

Instead of comparing feature lists, check these four points on any app. They tell you more than any review.

1. **Is there a profile field for your relationship structure?** If your situation only fits in free text, the app was not built for you. You will be explaining forever.
2. **Can you make existing partnerships visible?** A linking feature removes the same repeated explanation and makes transparency the default rather than a confession.
3. **How granular is visibility?** Can you control who sees you? Do your photos surface in search engines? For anyone who cannot be out at work or with family, this is the decisive point – more on that in [Privacy in polyamory](/en/blog/polyamory-privacy/).
4. **How many active profiles are near you?** The most honest test: sign up, set the radius to 25 kilometres, start swiping. Twenty minutes tells you whether the app is worth it where you live.

## One thing that matters more than the app: searching as a couple

If you are an existing couple looking for a third person together, the choice of app is secondary. What matters is how you show up. Joint couple profiles aiming to find one woman for both partners are known in the community as unicorn hunting, and they meet scepticism for good reason: the third person rarely has the same rights as the other two.

That does not mean you cannot search together. It means you should disclose from the start which agreements, veto rights and expectations exist in the background. The full background is in [Unicorn hunting: why the search for a third so often goes wrong](/en/blog/unicorn-hunting-polyamory/).

## What happens once the app has done its job

Dating apps solve exactly one problem: finding people. Once two matches have turned into two ongoing relationships, their usefulness ends abruptly. ## Frequently asked questions

**Which dating app is best for polyamory?**
There is no single best app, only one that fits your situation. Feeld and #open are built for non-monogamous dating from the ground up, which makes them the obvious starting point. OkCupid has far greater reach and is worth it if you live outside a major city. In practice, many polyamorous people run two apps side by side.

**Can I date as a polyamorous person on Tinder, Hinge or Bumble?**
Yes, but with friction. These apps are designed around finding one person, and their profile fields barely account for several simultaneous relationships. You can put your situation in the free text, but expect to explain yourself more often and to meet more rejection than on an app that assumes non-monogamy exists.

**What is the difference between a dating app and an app for organising relationships?**
A dating app helps you find people. Once those turn into several ongoing relationships, its purpose ends. Running those relationships – scheduling, agreements, safer sex, noticing who needs attention – takes a different kind of tool.

**How safe is my data on a poly dating app?**
Relationship and sexuality data is among the most sensitive there is. Before signing up, check the current privacy disclosure in the App Store, whether the app exposes profile photos to search engines, and whether it offers a discretion mode. For anyone who cannot be out at work or with family, this is the single most important selection criterion.

**Should a couple search together or separately?**
Separately, almost always. Joint couple profiles looking for a third person are known as unicorn hunting and meet justified scepticism. If you do search together, be transparent from the first message about what expectations and veto rights exist in the background.
