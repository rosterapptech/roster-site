**In short:** Almost everywhere, marriage is limited to two people. For every other relationship, whatever is not explicitly arranged does not exist legally. In an emergency, a partner without authorisation gets no information; without a will, they inherit nothing; and in most countries a child cannot have more than two legal parents. Much of this can be protected privately, though: with powers of attorney and advance directives, wills, a lease or deed with everyone’s names on it, and a written cohabitation or partnership agreement.

*As of September 2026. This article is a general overview. Law differs significantly between countries and, in the US, between states. It is not legal advice – for your specific situation, talk to a lawyer who knows your local law.*

Polyamorous relationships are legal in most Western countries. Nobody needs permission to love several people or to live with them. The problem is a different one: the law essentially knows one form of romantic relationship, marriage between two people. Rights to information, inheritance, tax advantages and insurance benefits are attached to it. If you love outside that form, you face emergencies without any of it.

This article shows where the gaps are and how polyamorous people can close them, area by area. Where we give examples, they come mainly from the UK, the US and Germany.

## The starting point: the law thinks in couples

Being married to more than one person at a time is not possible in virtually any Western legal system, and bigamy is a criminal offence in many, including the UK and every US state. For polyamorous constellations that means: at most one relationship per person can be a marriage. Legally, all the others are relationships between strangers.

That has two consequences worth knowing:

1. **Marriage creates a hierarchy automatically.** Even if you want to live [non-hierarchically](/en/blog/hierarchical-vs-non-hierarchical-polyamory/), a married person has a special legal status: at the hospital, in inheritance, often in tax and health insurance. You cannot agree that away, but you can partly balance it out.
2. **Doing nothing is also a decision.** Without your own arrangements, the default rules apply – and they provide almost nothing for unmarried partners.

A few places have started to recognise more than two partners in limited ways. Individual US cities, for example, have introduced domestic partnership rules that allow more than two people, mainly for municipal purposes. These are exceptions with narrow effects, not a general legal framework.

## Health and emergencies: powers of attorney and advance directives

This is where the gap shows up fastest. After an accident or a serious diagnosis, doctors may only share information with people who are entitled to it. Spouses often have default rights or are treated as next of kin in practice. Unmarried partners usually have neither.

**What helps:**

- **A healthcare power of attorney.** It authorises one or more people to make decisions for you when you cannot. Depending on where you live, it may be called a lasting power of attorney, a healthcare proxy or something similar, and some countries require it to be registered before it can be used. Many systems let you appoint several partners and decide whether each can act alone or only jointly.
- **A release of medical confidentiality.** Without it, doctors may not be allowed to tell your partners anything, even if they are at your bedside.
- **An advance directive or living will.** It sets out which treatments you want or refuse in specific situations and takes pressure off the people who have to decide for you.
- **A financial power of attorney.** Covers bank accounts, bills and contracts. Many banks also want their own forms.

Important for polyamorous constellations: talk beforehand about who should decide what. Several authorised partners meeting for the first time at a hospital with different ideas is a hard situation. A good relationship between [metamours](/en/blog/metamour-relationships/) pays off very practically here.

## Inheritance: wills and tax

Intestacy rules – what happens when someone dies without a will – provide for spouses and blood relatives almost everywhere. An unmarried partner inherits nothing, even after decades together.

**What helps:**

- **A will.** You can leave assets to anyone you choose. Formal requirements vary: some countries accept handwritten wills, others require witnesses or a notary. A will that does not meet local requirements may be invalid, so this is worth getting right.
- **Beneficiary designations.** Life insurance, pensions and some bank or retirement accounts let you name who receives the money. These designations often take priority over a will, so keep them up to date.
- **Joint ownership with survivorship.** In common-law countries, property held as joint tenants passes automatically to the surviving owners. As tenants in common, each person’s share goes to their estate instead. Which one you choose matters a lot in a multi-partner household.

**Watch for compulsory shares.** In many civil-law countries, including Germany, children, parents or a spouse are entitled to a minimum share of the estate that a will cannot remove. If you are married and want to provide for other partners, you need to plan around that.

**Tax is often the big disadvantage.** Many countries exempt transfers between spouses from inheritance tax but treat other partners as unrelated people. Spouses often inherit tax-free or with large allowances, while unmarried partners are taxed like anyone else. If significant assets are involved, get tax advice.

## Housing: leases and property

Many polyamorous constellations live together, fully or partly. Legally, what matters is whose name is on the paperwork.

**Renting:**

- **Everyone on the lease.** Whoever is named has full tenant rights but is also liable for the whole rent. That is the most secure option for everyone who lives there long term.
- **Subletting or lodging.** If only one person is the tenant, others usually need the landlord’s consent and have much weaker rights. If that tenant leaves or dies, the others may lose their home.
- **Succession rights.** Some jurisdictions let a household member take over a tenancy after the tenant’s death, but the rules differ widely and often favour a spouse.

**Buying:** If you buy property together, decide how ownership is split and what happens if someone moves out, separates or dies. A written agreement alongside the deed – sometimes called a declaration of trust in the UK – records who contributed what and who gets what. Talk to a lawyer before signing.

## Children: two legal parents, several caregivers

Most legal systems recognise at most two legal parents. A third person who helps raise a child has no parental status, however close the bond.

A few jurisdictions are exceptions. Some US states and Canadian provinces allow more than two legal parents under specific conditions, for example with assisted reproduction and a written agreement made before conception. If this matters to you, check the exact requirements where you live.

**What helps elsewhere:**

- **Written authorisations for everyday matters.** Legal parents can authorise other caregivers to pick the child up from school, speak to doctors or attend parents’ evenings. Parental responsibility itself usually cannot be handed over by private agreement.
- **Naming a guardian in your will.** Parents can say whom they want to care for the child if both die. For poly families this is often the single most important step to make sure a trusted caregiver is considered.
- **Providing financially.** If a non-parent wants to provide for a child, that goes through a will, trust or insurance – with the tax issues described above.

How poly families handle daily life with children is covered in [Polyamory and parenting](/en/blog/polyamory-parenting/).

## Money and living together: the partnership agreement

For unmarried people there is often no family law that applies on separation: no division of marital property, no maintenance, no default rules about household goods. That is freedom and risk at the same time.

A written **cohabitation or partnership agreement** can fill the gap. It can cover, for example:

- how shared costs are split,
- who owns what you buy,
- what happens when someone moves out or a relationship ends,
- how joint loans or contracts are handled.

In most places such an agreement falls under general contract law and can be made between more than two people. Whether it is enforceable, and in which parts, depends on local law – another reason to have it checked. For fair everyday arrangements, see [Finances in polyamory](/en/blog/finances-polyamory/).

## Checklist: where to start

You do not have to do everything at once. This order has proven useful:

1. **Healthcare power of attorney and medical confidentiality release** for everyone who should be there for you in an emergency.
2. **Advance directive**, so nobody has to guess in a crisis.
3. **Will**, as soon as assets, a home or children are involved. With children, include a guardian nomination.
4. **Beneficiary designations** on insurance and pensions, checked and up to date.
5. **Check the lease or deed:** whose name is on it, and whose should be?
6. **Partnership agreement** if you live together or make larger purchases together.
7. **Legal advice** as soon as property, significant assets, children or more than one country are involved.

Above all: talk about who should be there for whom when it matters. It is an uncomfortable question, but it is one of the most committed questions people in a relationship can ask each other.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is polyamory legal?**
In most Western countries, yes. Having several consensual relationships at the same time, and living together, is not a crime. What is not possible almost anywhere is being married to more than one person at once – bigamy is a criminal offence in the UK and every US state, for example. The practical issue is that the law does not recognise the other relationships as family.

**Can my partner get information at the hospital if we are not married?**
Not automatically in most places. Spouses often have default rights or are at least treated as next of kin in practice; unmarried partners usually are not. A healthcare power of attorney (also called a lasting power of attorney or healthcare proxy, depending on the country) plus a release of medical confidentiality closes that gap, and many jurisdictions let you name more than one person.

**Do unmarried partners inherit?**
Generally not without a will. Intestacy rules in most countries only provide for spouses and blood relatives. With a will you can leave assets to any partner, but they often do not get the tax exemptions a spouse would. In some countries, including Germany, children and spouses are also entitled to a compulsory share that a will cannot remove.

**Can a child have more than two legal parents?**
In most countries, no. A small number of jurisdictions, for example some US states and Canadian provinces, allow more than two legal parents under certain conditions. Elsewhere, additional caregivers can be involved through written authorisations for everyday matters and named as preferred guardians in a will.

**Do we need a lawyer?**
Not for everything: in many places you can draft a simple power of attorney or will yourself. But for property, larger estates, children or anything cross-border, advice from a lawyer who knows your local law is strongly recommended. This article is a general overview and not legal advice.
