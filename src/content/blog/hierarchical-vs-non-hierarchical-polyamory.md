Few questions spark as much discussion in polyamorous communities as this one: should relationships have a fixed rank, or should each connection be evaluated on its own terms? Both camps have solid arguments, and both models can work well – or fall apart. This article lays out the differences without declaring either side "correct."

## What hierarchical polyamory means

In hierarchical polyamory, there is an explicitly named rank between relationships. Usually there is a "primary partner" or "nesting partner" – someone you live with, share finances with, or make long-term life decisions alongside. Other relationships are labeled "secondary" and often have less say in major decisions. Veto power is a common feature: the primary partner can restrict or even end a new relationship if it's seen as threatening the primary one. This model often grew out of practical necessity – sharing a home, kids, or a joint bank account inevitably means making decisions that affect other relationships too.

## What non-hierarchical polyamory means

Non-hierarchical polyamory – sometimes bordering on relationship-anarchist thinking – deliberately does without a fixed rank. Every relationship is evaluated on its own merits, not by its status relative to others. There's no automatic "first among equals," no veto power, no relationship that is definitionally more important than another. That doesn't mean every relationship has to look the same – one connection can be more intense, closer, or take up more time than another. The difference is that this variation arises organically from the people involved, rather than from a predetermined structure.

## Pros and cons of hierarchy

Hierarchical models offer clarity and security, especially for couples transitioning from monogamy into polyamory. They protect existing commitments like marriage, shared kids, or a shared home. The downside: veto power can feel, to the "secondary" person, like a structural devaluation – their relationship is by definition more vulnerable, no matter how deep the actual emotional connection is. People who spend a long time in the secondary position often report never quite feeling secure.

## Pros and cons of non-hierarchy

Non-hierarchical models respect the independence of each relationship and avoid the feeling of being squeezed into a ranking. They tend to suit people who are solo-poly or relationship-anarchist oriented from the start. The downside: without clear agreements, uncertainty can creep in around who gets priority during conflicts or when resources – time, energy, attention – are scarce. Practical questions, like who gets informed during an illness or who has a say in a move, also stay less resolved when there are no predefined roles.

## The invisible hierarchy: de facto vs. prescriptive

A key point that often gets overlooked: even explicitly non-hierarchical relationships almost always develop some degree of hierarchy – simply through the reality of time, living space, and finances. Someone who lives with a partner automatically spends more time with that person. Someone who shares kids with a partner inevitably makes more joint decisions with them. This is called de facto hierarchy – it emerges from life circumstances, not from a conscious rule. The difference from prescriptive hierarchy lies in flexibility: de facto hierarchy can shift as life circumstances change, while a prescriptive hierarchy often remains fixed as a rule even when it no longer matches how people actually feel. Honestly naming that certain practical priorities exist is often more relieving than pretending every relationship is structured identically.

## How the choice shapes communication and agreements

The model you choose changes what couples and networks even need to talk about. Hierarchical structures require clear agreements about which decisions the primary partner gets a say in, and where the line sits between legitimate influence and overreaching veto power. Non-hierarchical structures, on the other hand, require more individual negotiation per relationship – each connection needs its own agreements rather than deferring to one overarching rule. ## Common questions

**Is hierarchical polyamory automatically bad for secondaries?**
No, not automatically. Many people are consciously and happily in a secondary role. It becomes a problem when the hierarchy is used to systematically override the secondary person's needs.

**Can you switch from hierarchical to non-hierarchical?**
Yes, this happens often as relationships deepen or life circumstances change. Such a shift usually requires open conversations with everyone involved.

**Does non-hierarchical mean every relationship gets equal time?**
No. Non-hierarchical means no relationship is structurally placed above another – how much time is actually invested follows from individual needs and life circumstances.

**Does every hierarchical relationship include veto power?**
No. Some hierarchical models define a primary partner without granting them veto power over new relationships. That's a separate decision that should be discussed explicitly.
