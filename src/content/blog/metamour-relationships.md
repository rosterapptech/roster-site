**In short:** A metamour is your partner's partner – someone you don't have a romantic relationship with yourself, but who's part of your extended [polycule](/en/blog/what-is-a-polycule/). How close this relationship gets is entirely open: anywhere from close friendship to "we barely know each other" is normal and valid. What matters isn't closeness itself, but respect, clear communication, and realistic expectations.

Anyone new to polyamory tends to run into the term **metamour** pretty quickly – and the question of what to actually do with this person. Do you have to like them? Do you even have to meet? And what if you simply can't stand each other? This article breaks down the key metamour dynamics and shows how the relationship can feel easier for everyone involved.

## What exactly is a metamour?

The term combines "meta" (beyond) and "amour" (love): a person beyond your own romantic relationship who's still connected to you through a shared partner. Example: you're with Anna, and Anna is also with Ben – then Ben is your metamour, and you're his. You don't have to like each other, be friends, or even know each other personally. The relationship simply exists because you're both connected to the same person.

## The spectrum of metamour relationships

There's no "right" or "wrong" here – just different models that work depending on the people and life circumstances involved:

- **Kitchen table polyamory:** Everyone involved figuratively sits at the same kitchen table – you know each other, possibly spend time together, and have a warm to friendly relationship with your metamours.
- **Parallel polyamory:** The relationships run alongside each other without overlapping. You know about each other and respect one another, but there's little to no direct contact.
- **Garden party polyamory:** A middle ground – you occasionally meet at bigger events (birthdays, celebrations) but don't maintain close everyday contact.

None of these models is "more advanced" than another. Introverted people, or anyone with limited social capacity, often do better with parallel structures – while others draw a lot of energy from genuine kitchen-table contact.

## Common challenges with metamours

**Comparison and feelings of competition.** It's human to unconsciously compare yourself to your metamour – who gets more time, who gets mentioned more often, who "wins" in some vague sense. Taking that feeling seriously without acting on it is an important step. For more on handling these feelings, see [Jealousy in Polyamory](/en/blog/jealousy-polyamory/).

**Using the shared partner as a go-between.** Some people only communicate with each other through the shared partner instead of directly. That overloads the person in the middle and often distorts what actually needs to be said. Direct contact – even if it feels awkward at first – relieves everyone in the long run.

**Mismatched expectations around closeness.** If one person wants a close friendship with the metamour and the other prefers distance, it can lead to frustration. The only fix is naming your own wishes clearly, without forcing them onto the other person.

**Loyalty conflicts.** Sometimes it feels like you have to choose between solidarity with your own partner and fairness toward the metamour. In most cases, that's a false either-or – good three-way communication resolves most of these situations.

## How to build a good metamour relationship

- **Clarify your own expectations before communicating them.** Do you actually want friendship, or is friendly respect enough? Be honest with yourself before you head into the conversation.
- **Never force closeness.** A warm but distant relationship is a perfectly legitimate goal – "we don't have to be friends" is a healthy thing to say.
- **Communicate directly instead of through detours.** As soon as a topic directly concerns the metamour, a short conversation between the two of you is almost always better than relaying it through the shared partner.
- **Respect boundaries – including unspoken ones.** Not everyone wants to be informed about the details of the other relationship. Ask instead of assuming.
- **Don't put the shared partner in a bind.** Avoid asking your partner to "choose" between you and the metamour – that puts unfair structural pressure on the relationship.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a metamour, explained simply?**
A metamour is your partner's partner – someone you don't have a relationship with yourself, but who's connected to you through a shared partner.

**Do I have to like my metamour or be friends with them?**
No. There are many valid models, from close friendship (kitchen table polyamory) to barely any contact (parallel polyamory). What matters is mutual respect, not closeness at any cost.

**What if I don't like my metamour?**
A warm but distant relationship is entirely enough. Direct, respectful communication about boundaries matters more than forced likability.

**What's the best way to communicate with a metamour?**
Directly, wherever possible – rather than routing messages through the shared partner. That takes pressure off everyone involved and avoids misunderstandings.

**What's the difference between kitchen table and parallel polyamory?**
In kitchen table polyamory, everyone involved – including metamours – maintains a close, friendly relationship. In parallel polyamory, the relationships run side by side with little to no direct contact between metamours.
