**In short:** A hinge is the person in the middle of a V relationship: you have two partners who are not a couple themselves. The role comes with particular responsibility, because information, scheduling and often tension run through you. It works well when you nurture each relationship on its own terms, plan transparently, do not act as a translator between your partners and do not offload criticism of one onto the other.

Anyone dating two people at the same time for the first time is usually a hinge without knowing the word. At first it feels simple: two people, two relationships. Only over time do you notice that you are standing at a point where a lot converges: calendars, expectations, insecurities and sometimes conflicts that are not really yours.

This article explains what the role involves, where it gets tiring and which habits help you stay fair.

## What a hinge is

In a V structure you are the connecting piece: you are with A and with B, but A and B are not a couple. A and B are [metamours](/en/blog/metamour-relationships/) to each other, meaning your partner's partner.

Hinges also exist in larger networks. In a [polycule](/en/blog/what-is-a-polycule/) of five people, several people are often hinges at once, each for different connections. The core questions stay the same.

Important: hinge is a position, not a ranking. The term says nothing about which relationship is longer, closer or more important. Whether you live your relationships [hierarchically or non-hierarchically](/en/blog/hierarchical-vs-non-hierarchical-polyamory/) is a separate decision.

## Why the role can be tiring

As a hinge you know more than anyone else. You know when A's birthday is and that B is having a hard week. You know both calendars, both moods, both wishes. That is practical and a burden at the same time.

Typical pressures:

- **Scheduling pressure.** Both partners want time, and you are the person who has to say no or reschedule.
- **Expectations from two sides.** What A wants sometimes collides with what B needs, and both look to you.
- **Feelings that run through you.** If A feels jealous of B, you feel it first. You often feel guilty even though nobody did anything wrong.
- **Loyalty conflicts.** If A tells you something critical about B, you are instantly standing between two people who matter to you.

That does not make the role bad. Many hinges find it enriching. But it needs deliberate habits, otherwise it becomes a constant strain.

## What is your responsibility and what is not

Perhaps the most important clarity for a hinge is where their own responsibility ends.

**Your responsibility:**

- Keeping agreements with both partners, even when it is inconvenient.
- Communicating plans early and transparently instead of rescheduling at short notice.
- Not passing on information about one partner to the other in a distorted way or without being asked.
- Nurturing each relationship on its own, with its own time, rituals and conversations.
- Being honest when you are reaching your limits.

**Not your responsibility:**

- Whether your metamours like each other.
- That nobody ever feels jealous. [Jealousy](/en/blog/jealousy-polyamory/) belongs to the person who feels it, even if you can soften it with care and attention.
- Solving every conflict between your partners.

This line sounds harsh but relieves everyone. If you try to be responsible for the feelings of two people at once, you will inevitably fail and become unfair to yourself.

## The typical traps

**1. The translator.** A tells you something that is really meant for B, and you pass it on, softened a little, of course. B replies, you translate back. After a few rounds nobody knows exactly what was meant. If your metamours have something to clear up and both are open to it, a direct conversation is almost always better.

**2. The outlet.** You have a fight with A and tell B all about it. That is human, but it shapes B's picture of A without A being able to respond. Find someone outside the polycule to vent to.

**3. The comparison.** "It's so much easier with B" is a sentence that damages a relationship very quickly, even if it is only thought and never said. Every relationship has its own logic.

**4. The buffer.** You swallow dissatisfaction so as not to burden either partner, and after three months you are exhausted. Being honest about your capacity is not selfish; it is the precondition for both relationships holding up. The article on [polysaturation](/en/blog/polysaturation/) goes deeper.

**5. The NRE tilt.** When one of the relationships is new, the infatuation pulls attention away. The longer-standing partner often notices before you do. More on that in [keeping NRE in balance](/en/blog/nre-balance/).

## How to stay fair as a hinge

**Shared visibility of plans.** Much tension comes not from too little time but from uncertainty. If both partners know when you are where, nobody feels passed over. How to solve this in practice is covered in [scheduling multiple relationships](/en/blog/scheduling-multiple-relationships/).

**Separate conversations, separate rituals.** A regular evening, a shared hobby, regular [check-ins](/en/blog/relationship-check-ins-polyamory/): every relationship needs something that belongs only to it.

**Discretion by default.** What a partner tells you in confidence stays with you unless you have agreed otherwise. It is also worth clarifying in advance what you may share about a relationship.

**No forced closeness.** Whether your metamours want to get to know each other is their decision. Some polycules live [kitchen table, others parallel](/en/blog/kitchen-table-vs-parallel-polyamory/); both are fine.

**Address conflicts in the right place.** If A has a problem with one of your agreements with B, that is a topic between you and A. If A has a problem with B as a person, it belongs between A and B, if anywhere.

**A place for yourself.** As a hinge especially, it pays to sort through your own feelings regularly before you go into conversations.

## When the metamours are in contact

In some V relationships the metamours get along well, and sometimes even become friends. That takes a huge load off the hinge, but brings its own questions:

- What do the two of them tell each other about you, and are you okay with that?
- What happens if one relationship ends but the metamours' friendship stays?
- Will plans now be discussed by all three, or still through you?

Clear up these questions early and without pressure. The [guide to metamour relationships](/en/blog/metamour-relationships/) helps.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a hinge in polyamory?**
A hinge is the person who is with two partners who are not in a relationship with each other. The three form a V shape, and the two outer people are metamours to each other. The term describes a position in the relationship network, not a hierarchy.

**What is a hinge responsible for?**
A hinge is responsible for their own relationships: keeping agreements, being transparent about scheduling, not passing on information in a distorted way and not using one partner as an outlet for frustration about the other. A hinge is not responsible for how the metamours feel about each other.

**Does a hinge have to mediate between metamours?**
No, not permanently. When there is tension, it is often better for the metamours to talk directly, if both want to. A hinge who translates every message quickly becomes a bottleneck and unintentionally distorts what was said.

**What if the metamours do not like each other?**
Metamours do not have to like each other. It is enough if they can be respectful or simply stay out of each other's way. The hinge should not push anyone into closeness and should keep both relationships separate, more parallel than kitchen table.
