**In short:** Studies from the US and Europe suggest that roughly 4 to 5 percent of people are currently living in some form of ethically non-monogamous relationship, while significantly more – in some surveys over 20 percent – say they could generally imagine such a relationship structure or have tried it at some point. Polyamory is therefore not a niche phenomenon among a tiny few, but a lived reality for millions of people, even if public perception often underestimates it.

How many people actually live polyamorously? This question comes up surprisingly often – and the answer is more complicated than a single number would suggest.

## What the research shows

Various surveys and studies from recent years, mostly from the US, paint a fairly consistent picture:

- **Currently living in ENM relationships:** Surveys consistently land in the range of about 4 to 5 percent of surveyed adults who are, at the time of the survey, in a consensually non-monogamous relationship.
- **Ever tried it:** Considerably more people – in some studies, over a fifth of respondents – say they've practiced some form of ENM at some point in their lives, even if they currently live monogamously.
- **Openness to the idea:** Even larger is the share of people who say they could theoretically imagine a non-monogamous relationship, even without having tried it in practice.

## Why exact numbers are hard to pin down

**Definitional differences.** Depending on whether a study lumps polyamory, open relationships, and swinging together or measures them separately, results can differ substantially. More on the terms in [Ethical Non-Monogamy](/en/blog/ethical-non-monogamy).

**Underreporting due to reticence.** Not everyone living polyamorously is willing to disclose that in a survey – especially in regions or professions where non-monogamy is stigmatized or even comes with real disadvantages.

**Fluid transitions.** Some people move between monogamous and non-monogamous phases of their lives, which complicates a single-point-in-time snapshot.

## Who lives polyamorously? What studies show about demographics

Research suggests ENM relationships occur across practically every age group, gender, and sexual orientation – though some groups (like younger people and people from the LGBTQ+ community) tend to be overrepresented in surveys. Importantly: polyamory doesn't correlate significantly with lower relationship satisfaction – studies on relationship quality frequently show comparable, or in some aspects even slightly better, scores on communication and conflict resolution compared to monogamous comparison groups.

## What this means for you

These numbers are useful mainly in one respect: they show that people living polyamorously aren't a tiny fringe group, but part of a growing, increasingly visible spectrum of relationship structures. Anyone living polyamorously themselves, or considering it, is far less alone than public perception sometimes suggests. A good starting point for anyone new to this space is [Polyamory for Beginners](/en/blog/polyamory-for-beginners).

## Growing visibility, not necessarily growing numbers

It's more likely that the increasing social visibility of polyamory – through media, social media, and more open discussions – leads to more honest survey answers rather than an actual increase in the practice itself. In other words: polyamory isn't necessarily becoming more common, just increasingly visible.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many people live polyamorously?**
Studies suggest roughly 4 to 5 percent of adults currently live in some form of ethically non-monogamous relationship – considerably more have tried it at some point in their lives.

**Is polyamory a new phenomenon?**
Non-monogamous relationship structures have existed historically and culturally for a long time. What's new is mainly the public visibility and the use of the term "polyamory" itself.

**Are polyamorous relationships less stable than monogamous ones?**
Studies show no clear decline – some even show comparable or slightly better scores on communication and conflict resolution.

**Why is it so hard to pin down exact numbers on polyamory?**
Because of differing definitions, underreporting due to reticence in sensitive surveys, and fluid transitions between monogamous and non-monogamous life phases.

**Is polyamory actually becoming more common?**
It's more likely that social visibility is increasing, leading people to answer surveys more honestly, rather than the actual practice increasing sharply.
