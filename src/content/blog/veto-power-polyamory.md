**In short:** Veto power is an arrangement where one person (usually within an existing couple) has the right to end their partner's relationship with a third person. It's considered one of the most controversial concepts in the polyamory community, because it completely ignores the third person's autonomy and replaces genuine consent with control. Most experienced polyamorous people advise against it – not because boundaries are inherently wrong, but because a veto is the wrong kind of boundary.

Few terms spark discussion in the polyamory community as quickly as **veto power**. Anyone new to the field runs into it sooner or later – usually paired with a warning to avoid it.

## What veto power actually means

A classic veto power works like this: one person in an existing (often called "primary") relationship can demand that their partner end another relationship – without the third person having had any say in that decision. The veto can be formally agreed on, or exist informally, through the unspoken expectation of "if I really want it, you'll end it."

## Why veto power is so controversial

**It ignores the third person's autonomy.** The person whose relationship gets ended usually had no voice in the decision – their feelings and investment are effectively nullified.

**It replaces consent with control.** Instead of all three people negotiating together what works for everyone, one person decides on the relationship of the other two. More on the difference between control and genuine agreement in [Rules vs. Agreements in Polyamory](/blog/polyamory-rules-agreements).

**It displaces the actual problem.** The desire for a veto usually comes from insecurity or jealousy within the existing relationship. But the veto doesn't address that insecurity – it just eliminates its source from the outside, without working through the underlying feeling. More on that in [Jealousy in Polyamory](/blog/jealousy-polyamory).

**It creates structural inequality.** Relationships with veto power automatically establish a hierarchy in which one connection is revocable at any time and the other isn't – regardless of how deep the "revocable" relationship actually is.

## Is every boundary a veto?

No. The difference lies in what an agreement actually decides:

- **Legitimate boundary:** "I don't want you to have dates in our shared home." This concerns your own living situation.
- **Veto power:** "I decide whether you're allowed to have this relationship at all." This directly concerns another person's autonomy.

Boundaries that protect your own life are legitimate. Agreements meant to control another person's behavior or feelings are not.

## Fairer alternatives to veto power

**Open conversations instead of unilateral decisions.** If a relationship causes discomfort, that deserves an honest three-way conversation rather than a unilateral ending.

**Agreed-upon check-ins.** Regular, visible conversations about how each connection feels, instead of an implicit threat looming in the background.

**Naming boundaries instead of dictating control.** Stating your own needs ("I need more time with you") instead of determining the other person's behavior.

## Why some couples still hold onto it

Veto power feels like security to some, especially early in opening up a relationship. That security is deceptive, though: it just shifts the real trust problem instead of solving it, and it builds in a structural unfairness toward new partners that rarely stays healthy long-term.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is veto power in polyamory?**
Veto power lets one person end their partner's relationship with a third person, without that third person having had any say in the decision.

**Why is veto power problematic?**
It completely ignores the third person's autonomy and replaces genuine consent with unilateral control.

**Are boundaries in polyamory inherently bad?**
No. Boundaries that protect your own life (like where dates take place) are legitimate. It becomes problematic when an agreement directly controls another person's autonomy.

**What can you agree on instead of veto power?**
Open three-way conversations, regular check-ins, and naming your own needs instead of controlling someone else's behavior.

**Why do some couples still agree to veto power anyway?**
It feels like security in the short term, especially early in opening up a relationship – but it usually doesn't solve the actual trust problem.
