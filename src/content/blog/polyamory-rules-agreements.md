**In short:** Polyamory often distinguishes between "rules" and "agreements." Rules are usually set unilaterally by one person (or an existing couple) for others, restricting their autonomy – like "you're not allowed to fall in love with anyone else." Agreements, by contrast, are negotiated among everyone involved, can be revised, and respect each person's autonomy. Functioning polycules rely almost exclusively on agreements rather than rigid rules.

Few topics generate as much discussion in polyamory as the question of which arrangements between multiple partners are actually reasonable and fair. The distinction between a "rule" and an "agreement" might sound like semantics at first – but it's genuinely one of the core building blocks of healthy, long-term-functioning polycules.

## Rules vs. agreements: the key difference

**Rules** are usually set by an existing couple (or a single person) for new or existing connections, often without the affected third person having a say in the negotiation. Typical examples: "You're not allowed to have overnight stays with other partners," "We decide together who you're allowed to date," or the infamous veto power.

**Agreements**, on the other hand, are made jointly, among everyone affected by them. They're based on mutual respect rather than control, and they can be adjusted as circumstances or needs change. Example: "We tell each other about new sexual contacts for health reasons" – an agreement that protects everyone involved rather than restricting one person.

## Why rules often fail

**They restrict autonomy.** Rules that let one person dictate another's feelings or behavior (like banning falling in love) are essentially unenforceable – feelings can't be controlled by decree.

**They often come from fear rather than care.** Many rules are meant to ease jealousy or insecurity within an existing couple, but they only shift the actual problem onto the new person instead of addressing it directly. For more on this, see [Jealousy in Polyamory](/en/blog/jealousy-polyamory).

**They aren't owned by everyone.** Someone who wasn't allowed to help shape a rule tends to follow it less consistently – not out of spite, but because lacking a say rarely produces real buy-in.

## How good agreements come together

- **Include everyone affected.** An agreement that directly affects a third person should be negotiated with them, not decided over their head.
- **Name needs instead of dictating behavior.** Instead of "you're not allowed to stay overnight," try: "It matters to me to know in advance when I'll be home alone." The actual need becomes visible and negotiable.
- **Review regularly.** What fits today might not fit in a year. Good agreements get revisited and adjusted together at regular intervals.
- **Consequences instead of control.** Rather than trying to control someone else's behavior, clarify what happens if an agreement isn't kept – that places responsibility where it belongs.

## Examples of reasonable agreements

- **Health:** Regular STI testing and open communication about new sexual contacts – protects everyone without controlling anyone.
- **Time:** How much lead time is needed for new plans? More on that in [Scheduling Multiple Relationships](/en/blog/scheduling-multiple-relationships).
- **Communication:** How and when are new connections introduced to, or at least mentioned to, existing partners?
- **Closeness between metamours:** Is the polycule more [kitchen table or parallel](/en/blog/kitchen-table-vs-parallel-polyamory) – and how much contact does everyone actually want?

## When rules are still wanted

Some boundaries aren't unfair rules but legitimate personal limits – for instance, "I don't want dates to happen in our shared bed" directly concerns your own living situation and isn't an intrusion on someone else's autonomy. The difference lies in whether an arrangement protects your own life or tries to control another person's behavior and feelings.

## Frequently asked questions

**What's the difference between rules and agreements in polyamory?**
Rules are usually set unilaterally and restrict another person's autonomy. Agreements are negotiated jointly among everyone affected and respect each person's autonomy.

**Are rules in polyamory always bad?**
Not every arrangement that sounds like a rule is problematic. What matters is whether it protects your own life or tries to control another person's behavior and feelings.

**What is veto power, and why is it controversial?**
Veto power lets one person end their partner's other relationship. It's considered problematic because it completely ignores the third person's autonomy and replaces genuine consent with control.

**How do you negotiate fair agreements?**
By including everyone affected, naming needs instead of dictating behavior, and reviewing and adjusting the agreements together on a regular basis.

**How often should agreements be reviewed?**
There's no fixed rule, but many polycules set regular check-ins – every few months, or whenever there's a significant change in one of the relationships.
