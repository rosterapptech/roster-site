Spend enough time in poly spaces and you'll run into the term "unicorn hunting" – usually not said kindly. It describes a specific pattern in which an established couple (typically a man and a woman) look together for a bisexual woman to join their relationship. The term is a critique of a dynamic, not of the relationship shape itself. This article explains what it means, why the criticism is fair, and how couples who want to open up can do it better.

## What "unicorn hunting" actually means

The "unicorn" in this picture is the desired third person: bisexual, easygoing, and willing to fold herself into an already-established couple. The comparison to the mythical creature is deliberate – it points out that a person with exactly these qualities, in exactly this shape, rarely exists in reality.

The pattern behind it often looks like this: a couple decides together that they want to open up "for a woman." The rules – how often they'll meet, how much say the new person gets, whether she's allowed other relationships – are already set before the third person is even in the picture. She's searched for like an addition to something already finished, not like a person you build something new with together.

## Why the community uses the term critically

The criticism of unicorn hunting targets several recurring problems:

**Unequal power dynamics.** The couple makes the decisions; the new person steps into an existing system with rules already in place. This is what's often called "couple's privilege" – the couple relationship is automatically placed above every other connection, regardless of what the individuals involved actually need or want.

**The third person treated as a category, not an individual.** When an ad looks for "a bi woman for both of us," the person being sought is reduced to her sexual availability for two other people. Her own preferences, her own relationship pace, her own other potential partners often don't factor into the search at all.

**Unrealistic expectations.** It's common, implicitly or explicitly, to expect the new person to be exclusive with the couple – while the couple, of course, remains connected to each other. This asymmetry is rarely stated outright, but it's there in the background.

**Little to no say in the rules.** In many unicorn-hunting setups, the new person can't help shape the basic rules of the relationship, because those were already negotiated between the other two. She moves into a finished house, not one being built together.

## Healthy triads are still absolutely possible

Here's the important part: criticizing unicorn hunting does not mean three-person relationships are inherently a problem. Plenty of happy, stable triads and thruples exist, where three people genuinely meet each other as equals. The difference isn't the number of people involved – it's how the relationship comes together and is run. A triad where all three people help shape decisions from the start is something fundamentally different from a search where two people have already decided everything and the third person just needs to "fit."

## How couples can date together fairly

If you're a couple opening up and want to date together, a few concrete principles help you avoid unintentionally sliding into the unicorn-hunting pattern:

- **Treat a new person as a full individual with her own agency**, not as an addition to your existing relationship. She has her own needs, her own pace, and the right to say no.
- **Don't demand exclusivity you're not offering yourselves.** If you two remain each other's priority as a couple, it's unfair to expect the new person to give up other connections.
- **Involve her in making the rules**, instead of presenting her with finished ones. Agreements that affect a person should be made with her – not just about her.
- **Don't hold veto power over her autonomy.** For more on why veto arrangements are problematic in polyamory, see our article on [veto power in polyamory](/blog/veto-power-polyamory).
- **Ask yourselves honestly whether you're looking for a person or trying to fill a gap.** The difference between "we want to meet someone we can build something new with" and "we want someone who fits our existing life" is subtle, but it matters a lot.

## If you recognize the pattern in yourselves

Many couples slide into unicorn hunting without meaning to – simply because nobody told them there was another way, not out of bad intent. If you recognize the pattern in your own approach, you can change it: renegotiate rules openly, actually give the new person a say, reflect honestly on your own dynamic as a couple. ## Common questions

**Is unicorn hunting the same thing as a triad?**
No. A triad is a relationship shape involving three people. Unicorn hunting describes a specific, often unequal way a couple searches for a third person – the criticism targets the approach, not three-person relationships themselves.

**Why is the term "unicorn" used?**
Because the person being sought – bisexual, available, without her own demands, immediately fitting into the existing couple – rarely exists in that idealized form in real life.

**Is it wrong for a couple to open up together and look for a third person?**
Not inherently. It becomes a problem when the new person isn't treated as an equal with her own needs and a real say in the relationship.

**What is couple's privilege?**
It describes the pattern where the relationship between two people is automatically placed above every other connection, regardless of what the individuals involved actually need.
