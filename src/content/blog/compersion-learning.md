**In short:** Compersion is the joy you feel when someone you love is made happy by another relationship – often described as the emotional counterpart to jealousy. It is not a personality trait you either have or lack, but a skill that grows with practice, security and experience. And it does not rule out jealousy: most people feel both, sometimes in the very same moment.

In monogamous relationships there is no common word for it: the warm feeling that arises when your partner is happy with another person. In the polyamorous world it is called **compersion** – and it is often considered the emotional counterpart to jealousy.

The good news: compersion is not a personality trait that some people have and others do not. It is a skill you can practise and develop. This article shows you how.

## What exactly is compersion?

Compersion describes the joy and warmth you feel when someone you love is made happy by another relationship. Some describe it as "co-joy" – similar to how you might feel happy for a close friend who has just fallen in love, only directed towards your own partner.

Important: compersion is not a requirement and not proof of whether you are "good enough" at polyamory. Most people feel compersion sometimes and jealousy other times – sometimes even simultaneously. Feeling both at once is completely normal.

## Where does the word compersion come from?

The term is a coinage. It came out of the **Kerista Commune**, a communal living and relationship group in San Francisco that introduced the expression in the 1970s and 1980s – simply because English had no word for the feeling. From there it spread through the polyamorous community into wider use.

The closest everyday translation is "co-joy" or "sympathetic joy". The Buddhist concept of *mudita*, selfless joy at the happiness of others, is a close relative – a hint that the feeling itself is considerably older than the word for it.

## What compersion feels like: three examples

Compersion sounds abstract until you pin it to concrete moments:

- **At breakfast after the date.** Your partner tells you how lovely the evening was, and you notice yourself relaxing rather than tensing up as you listen. What you feel is not relief ("nothing happened"), but genuine gladness that they had a good night.
- **When your partner and your metamour laugh together.** You see the two of them and feel warmth instead of a sting. For many people that moment is the first conscious experience of compersion – more on this in the article on [metamour relationships](/en/blog/metamour-relationships/).
- **During your partner's new-relationship high.** Your partner is in the middle of [NRE](/en/blog/nre-balance/) with someone new. Compersion here does not mean the situation leaves you entirely untouched – it means you can see them glowing without reading it as a loss.

Notice something: in all three examples compersion is quiet rather than euphoric. People waiting for a big emotional wave tend to miss the small moments where it is already present.

## Why does compersion feel hard at first?

We are culturally trained to see love as a scarce resource: if my partner gives time and affection to someone else, less remains for me. This "scarcity thinking" gets in the way of compersion. The first step is therefore a cognitive one: recognising that your partner's happiness does not take anything away from you. Love does not work like a cake where every slice that goes out leaves less behind.

There is also a practical point that often gets overlooked: time *is* finite, even if love is not. Some of what feels like missing compersion is really a scheduling problem – too little reliable time together, too many last-minute reschedules. That layer can be fixed without working on your feelings at all.

## What compersion is not

Several misconceptions have attached themselves to the term, and they create more pressure than clarity:

- **Compersion is not an obligation.** Nobody owes it to anybody. A partner who demands compersion is not asking for a feeling, they are asking for a performance.
- **Compersion is not indifference.** Someone who feels nothing does not have compersion – they are uninvolved. Compersion presupposes connection, not disinterest.
- **Compersion is not proof of maturity.** It is not a test you pass, and it says nothing about how "advanced" anyone is at polyamory.
- **Compersion does not replace boundaries.** If an agreement was broken, your discomfort is not a compersion deficit but a legitimate reaction. More on this under agreements and concepts in the [glossary](/en/blog/polyamory-glossary/).

## 5 ways to strengthen compersion

### 1. Start with the easy moments

Compersion rarely appears first in the most difficult situation. Notice the moments where it already comes naturally – when your partner returns glowing from a lovely date, for example. Consciously registering these moments builds the feeling gradually.

### 2. Connect your partner's happiness to your own

When you love someone, you want them to do well. Compersion is essentially just this logic taken to its conclusion: their happiness is your concern too – regardless of where it comes from.

### 3. Work on your own foundation

Compersion comes more easily when you feel secure in yourself. Your own fulfilling relationships, hobbies, friendships and a stable sense of self-worth are the soil in which co-joy grows.

### 4. Talk about it

Ask your partner to share their positive experiences with you – to the extent that feels right for both of you. For many people, compersion becomes more tangible when they witness their partner's happiness rather than just imagining it.

### 5. Write down your moments

Compersion and jealousy often alternate. When you note both feelings down, you can see over time that the moments of co-joy increase – and that is enormously encouraging. In **Roster** you can do exactly that: record both jealousy and compersion in a private reflection journal and observe their development over weeks and months – locally and just for you.

## When compersion simply does not arrive

Sometimes you practise for weeks and nothing happens. Before concluding that something is wrong with you, three questions are worth asking:

**Is it really compersion that is missing – or an agreement that is not working?** Discomfort that shows up at the same point after every date usually points to an unclear arrangement rather than an emotional deficit.

**Are you simply short on security right now?** A freshly opened relationship, an acute crisis or unreliable time together are poor soil for co-joy. Stability comes first, compersion second.

**Are you putting yourself under pressure?** Compersion cannot be forced. Trying mostly produces shame – and shame blocks the very feeling you are aiming for. If jealousy is a persistent weight and conversations are not helping, outside support (poly-friendly counselling, for instance) is a shortcut, not a failure.

## Compersion and jealousy are not opponents

It is tempting to see compersion as the "end goal" and [jealousy](/en/blog/jealousy-polyamory/) as failure. It is more helpful to understand both as two sides of the same emotional work. Jealousy shows you a need; compersion shows you your connectedness. Dealing honestly with jealousy often creates the space in which compersion can grow at all.

In practice that means asking not "how do I get rid of this jealousy?" but "what does this feeling need right now?" The answer is often surprisingly concrete – a fixed evening together, a clearer agreement, a short message during a date. Once the need is met, there is room for everything else.

## Common questions

**What is compersion?**
Compersion is the joy you feel when someone you love is made happy by another relationship. It is often described as the emotional counterpart to jealousy.

**Can you learn compersion, or do you either have it or not?**
You can learn it. Compersion is a skill that grows with practice, self-confidence and experience.

**Is it bad if I do not feel compersion?**
No. Compersion is a wonderful bonus feeling, but not a prerequisite for successful polyamory. Honest engagement with your own feelings counts for more.

**Can I feel compersion and jealousy at the same time?**
Yes, in fact this is very common. Feelings are not either-or.

**Where does the word compersion come from?**
The term comes from the Kerista Commune in San Francisco, which coined it in the 1970s and 1980s because English had no word for the feeling. The closest everyday phrase is "co-joy".

**What is the difference between compersion and empathy?**
Empathy relates to someone else's pain or difficulty. Compersion is specifically the joy felt over a loved one's happiness in another relationship.

**Does compersion only exist in polyamorous relationships?**
The term comes from the polyamorous community, but the feeling does not. Joy at someone else's happiness that has nothing to do with you also happens in friendships, families and monogamous relationships.
