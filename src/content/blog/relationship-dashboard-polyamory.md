**In short:** A "relationship dashboard" isn't a finished product — it's an umbrella term for everything people in polyamory try to use to keep track of several relationships: DIY Notion templates, the network app polycul.es, the PYE Poly Calendar App, the Multiamory RADAR method, or repurposed CRM tools like Monica. Each of these approaches solves a slice of the problem, usually at the cost of privacy, effort, or both. **Roster** bundles what otherwise takes four or five separate tools into a single, locally-running app.

Anyone searching for "relationship dashboard" or "polyamory dashboard" rarely finds a ready-made app as the answer — mostly DIY solutions instead. That's no coincidence: for most use cases, finished software already exists, but for "organizing several concurrent, committed relationships," barely anything does. So many poly people reach for tools built for something else entirely and cobble together their own dashboard. This article looks at which approaches come up most often, what they're good at — and where their limits are.

## What a relationship dashboard is actually supposed to do

The underlying need is usually the same, regardless of which tool people pick: a central place for dates, agreements (boundaries), important details like STI testing dates, and an honest read on how each individual relationship is doing right now. With one relationship, memory handles this fine. With three, four, or five concurrent connections, it quickly becomes an organizational problem — which is exactly where the following approaches come in.

## The most common relationship-dashboard approaches compared

**Notion templates ("Polyamory Couple's Dashboard").** The most widespread option is a DIY or community-shared Notion template that maps origin stories, agreements, date ideas, and health info into databases. The upside: maximum customizability — everyone builds exactly what they need. The downside: Notion is a general-purpose tool for notes and projects, not built for relationships — links between people, a network view, or wellness tracking all have to be painstakingly constructed by hand, and the template demands ongoing upkeep.

**polycul.es.** A browser-based, visual web app for drawing your own polycule as an expandable relationship diagram — who's connected to whom, in what configuration. Good for pure network visualization, but with no agreement tracking, scheduling, or wellness features; it maps the structure, not the day-to-day.

**PYE Poly Calendar App.** A calendar built specifically for scheduling logistics across multiple partners. It solves the scheduling problem well, but covers neither agreements nor relationship health nor a network overview — a tool for one slice of the need, not the whole picture.

**Multiamory RADAR.** Not a digital tool but an established conversation framework (Review, Assess, Discuss, Adapt, Report) for regular relationship check-ins. Valuable for reflecting together, but it doesn't replace ongoing documentation — after the conversation, you still need somewhere to record what came out of it.

**Personal CRM systems (e.g. Monica).** Some people repurpose open-source CRM software to log notes, preferences, and interactions per person. Self-hosted, this can be privacy-friendly, but it's built for contact management, not relationship dynamics — concepts like metamours, agreements, or a polycule network are entirely missing and would have to be crudely recreated.

## The wall every DIY dashboard eventually hits

The pattern repeats: each of these approaches was originally built for something else — notes, contact management, pure visualization, or a conversation format — and then repurposed for polyamory. That works, but it costs effort: templates need maintaining, databases need linking, and several tools need running in parallel, because none of them covers agreements, network view, scheduling, and wellness tracking together. Anyone trying to cover all five areas quickly ends up juggling three or four different apps at once.

## Privacy: where does the data actually go?

This isn't a side note for a relationship dashboard — it's the core of the problem. Notion workspaces sit on third-party servers and are tied to an account, many CRM tools run in the vendor's cloud by default, and even browser-based network tools mostly process data server-side. For information like STI testing dates, safer-sex agreements, or metamours' names, that's a different category of data than a shopping list — and it deserves a tool that stays local from the ground up, rather than offering privacy as an afterthought setting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a relationship dashboard?**
A relationship dashboard isn't a finished product — it's an umbrella term for tools and methods people in polyamory use to keep track of several relationships, from Notion templates to network apps like polycul.es to conversation frameworks like Multiamory RADAR.

**Is a Notion template enough to organize polyamory?**
A Notion template can work as a starting point, but it requires ongoing upkeep since Notion is a general-purpose tool, not built specifically for relationship networks. Features like a network view or wellness tracking have to be built from scratch.

**How secure is my data in a relationship dashboard like Notion or a CRM app?**
It depends heavily on the tool. Many Notion workspaces and cloud CRM tools store data on third-party servers tied to an account. For sensitive information like STI testing dates or agreements, it's worth checking the current privacy listing yourself.

**What sets Roster apart from a DIY relationship dashboard?**
Roster combines the functions that otherwise require several separate tools — network view, agreements, scheduling, and wellness tracking — into a single app that runs entirely on your device, with no account and no cloud.
