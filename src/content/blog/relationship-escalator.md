**In short:** The relationship escalator is society’s default script for romantic relationships: meeting, exclusivity, becoming a couple, moving in, marriage, children. Each step automatically leads to the next, and a relationship counts as “serious” as long as it keeps moving up. Many non-monogamous people deliberately step out of that automatism: they decide step by step what they want to share with each person instead of riding the escalator.

Hardly anyone has ever consciously chosen the escalator. It is just there. After a few dates comes “so what are we?”, after a year “when are we moving in?”, and at some point relatives start asking about the wedding. Anyone who stops at a step has to explain themselves. Anyone who steps back supposedly lost a relationship.

For polyamorous people this script does not fit from the start, because one of its central steps – exclusivity – is missing. This article explains where the term comes from, which steps the escalator consists of, and how you shape relationships when you are not simply riding along.

## Where the term comes from

The term was popularised by US writer Amy Gahran. In her 2017 book *Stepping Off the Relationship Escalator* she describes the escalator as the set of expectations Western societies attach to a “proper” romantic relationship, and collects the experiences of people who deliberately deviate from it.

The image is well chosen. An escalator moves by itself. Once you are on it, you are carried upwards without taking a step. Getting off, on the other hand, is awkward, and walking the wrong way looks strange. That is exactly how many people experience the expectations placed on their relationships.

## The steps of the escalator

Gahran describes the escalator as a sequence of typical steps. Simplified, they look like this:

1. **Contact.** You meet, flirt, spend time together.
2. **Initiation.** Romantic or sexual interest is expressed; you date.
3. **Claiming.** You become “official” as a couple and usually promise exclusivity.
4. **Establishment.** The relationship develops routines: regular evenings, shared friends, meeting the families.
5. **Commitment.** You plan a future together, move in, merge finances.
6. **Conclusion.** Marriage or civil partnership, shared property, often children.

Underneath runs a silent rule: **the escalator only goes one way.** A relationship that steps back – from living together to living apart, say – is seen as failed or at least in crisis. And a relationship that never gets beyond step three is seen as not quite serious.

## Why the escalator does not fit everyone

The critique of the relationship escalator is not aimed at people who marry, move in together or have children. For many, that is exactly the right path. The problem is the automatism: the assumption that each step inevitably leads to the next, and that only this path produces a complete relationship.

That creates typical problems, in monogamous relationships too:

- **Decisions are not made, they happen.** You move in because “it’s time”, not because you both want to.
- **Relationships are measured by milestones.** A love without a shared home or wedding is seen as incomplete, however deep it is.
- **Change is read as failure.** If you realise separate homes work better, you have to justify it to everyone around you.
- **Relationships without upward movement get no room.** A loving, reliable connection that never wants to share more than one weekend a month has no place in the script.

In polyamory this becomes especially obvious. Several relationships cannot all reach the top at once – if only because most people can live with a limited number of others. Using the escalator as a yardstick automatically produces a ranking: the relationship with the shared home is “the real one”, all others are secondary. ## Stepping off does not mean giving up commitment

A common misunderstanding: stepping off the escalator means not wanting to commit. Often the opposite is true. If you are not simply riding along, you have to talk about every step and decide deliberately. That is more committed than an automatism, not less.

Stepping off can look very different:

- **Skipping individual steps.** A committed, years-long romantic relationship without a shared home. People who live this way often call it “living apart together”.
- **Reordering steps.** Raising a child together without being a romantic couple. Living together as close friends while romantic relationships happen elsewhere.
- **Staying on one step.** A relationship that has lived at the level of dates and trips together for years and is meant to stay that way.
- **Stepping back without failing.** Moving from a shared home back to separate flats because both need more space – and the relationship getting better for it.
- **Questioning the categories themselves.** Some people drop the distinction between “partner” and “friend” entirely and decide for each connection individually what they share. That is the core of [relationship anarchy](/en/blog/relationship-anarchy-vs-polyamory/).

A well-known way of stepping off is [solo polyamory](/en/blog/solo-polyamory/): people who have several relationships but deliberately do not want to move in, share finances or build a couple identity with any of them.

## The relationship smorgasbord: choosing step by step

A tool that is widespread in the non-monogamous community is the so-called relationship smorgasbord, sometimes called a relationship menu. Instead of a fixed staircase, there is a list of things you can share with a person or not. Each relationship picks its own combination.

Possible items on such a menu:

- **Time:** How often do we see each other? Do we stay over? Do we go on holiday together?
- **Living:** Separately, together, in the same city, within commuting distance?
- **Finances:** Completely separate, shared costs for outings, a joint account?
- **Visibility:** Do our families know each other? Are we visible as partners on social media? Do we introduce each other as partners?
- **Future:** Are we planning long-term? Do we want to have, raise or be part of children’s lives together?
- **Intimacy:** Romance, sexuality, physical closeness – in what form and to what extent?
- **Care:** Are we there for each other in an emergency? Do we look after each other when ill? Do we hold power of attorney for each other?
- **Daily life:** Household, pets, shared friend groups, routines.

The idea: each of these questions deserves its own answer. One relationship can go very far on care and stay completely separate on living arrangements. Another shares daily life and household but not sexuality. No combination is worth more or less than another.

These arrangements are easy to revisit in regular conversations. How to phrase agreements so they support relationships instead of constraining them is covered in [Rules vs. agreements in polyamory](/en/blog/polyamory-rules-agreements/).

## How to talk to partners about the escalator

The concept is useful, but it does not replace a conversation. Many conflicts arise because one person is on the escalator and the other is not – and neither of them realises it.

A few questions that work well for such a conversation:

- Which steps of the escalator do you want in general, and which not?
- Are there steps you only want to take with one particular person?
- What does commitment mean to you, if not moving in or marriage?
- How would you know that our relationship matters to me?
- Which expectations from the people around you do you feel, even if you do not share them yourself?

The last question matters most. Often it is not the partner who wants the escalator but the family, the friend group, or an inner picture of how relationships “normally” go.

If one partner clearly wants the escalator and you do not, that is not a problem good communication will dissolve. It is a genuine difference in life plans. Sometimes it can be balanced across different relationships – for instance if a partner lives with someone else. Sometimes it cannot. Acknowledging both honestly is better than a relationship in which one person is permanently waiting for the next step.

## Dealing with the people around you

If you live off the escalator, you get questions. “Don’t you want to move in together?”, “So it’s nothing serious?”, “When’s the wedding?” Such questions are rarely meant badly; they just follow the familiar script.

It helps to have a short, confident answer ready: “We’ve been together six years and deliberately live apart. It works brilliantly for us.” If you answer calmly and without justifying yourself, people often accept it quickly. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is the relationship escalator?**
The relationship escalator describes the socially expected sequence of a romantic relationship: meeting, falling in love, exclusivity, becoming a couple, moving in together, marriage, shared children or property. The term was popularised by the US writer Amy Gahran in her 2017 book Stepping Off the Relationship Escalator.

**Is the relationship escalator a bad thing?**
No. The escalator describes a path that is exactly right for many people. The criticism is not aimed at that path but at the assumption that it is the only one, or the only serious one. If you choose each step deliberately, you are no longer being carried by the escalator – you are walking it yourself.

**What does stepping off the relationship escalator mean?**
It means deliberately skipping, reordering or reshaping individual steps. For example, a committed romantic relationship without moving in, a long partnership without exclusivity, or a close relationship that never takes on the couple label. What matters is that each step is a decision, not an automatic next stage.

**Can you step off the escalator in a monogamous relationship?**
Yes. Monogamous couples also live apart, skip marriage or decide against children. The concept is not tied to non-monogamy. It is just especially visible in polyamory, because exclusivity – one of the steps – is off the table from the start.

**Are relationships off the escalator less serious?**
No. Seriousness shows in reliability, care and longevity, not milestones. A relationship that has lasted ten years without a shared home is no less serious than a marriage. It has simply found other ways to express commitment.
